# Style Pack: Lore — DM-Facing

**Domain:** DM-facing lore — encounter design notes, faction motivations, secret history, NPC psychology, adventure design rationale, and worldbuilding reference material
**Version:** 1.0
**Last updated:** 2026-03-28
**Related guides:** `guides/style-packs/`, `guides/canon/`, `styles/lore-player-facing.md`

---

## 1. Domain Overview

DM-facing lore is the working documentation of the world behind the curtain. It tells the Dungeon Master what to know, what to decide, and how to run the world with authority. It covers NPC motivations, faction dynamics, secret history, encounter design rationale, and operational context for any element the players might interact with.

The fundamental difference from player-facing lore: DM-facing material does not perform. It does not evoke. It informs and equips. The DM reads this before or during a session, often quickly. The writing must reward fast scanning without sacrificing operational depth.

DM-facing material has two failure modes: hiding key operational facts in atmospheric prose (which wastes the DM's time), and omitting motivation and limits for characters and factions (which leaves the DM unable to run them with coherence). Both are critical failures.

---

## 2. Reader and Purpose

**Reader:** The Dungeon Master. They have full access to world canon. They are not discovering the world — they are running it. They need to understand what things want, what they will do, and what will break them. They need this information quickly, in a form that survives session prep stress and mid-session reference.

**Secondary reader:** A different DM running the same material, who may not have built this world and needs the operational context to be explicit rather than assumed.

**Purpose:**
- Equip the DM to run NPCs as coherent, motivated agents — not puppets.
- Provide the factual and historical grounding that makes rulings consistent.
- Distinguish what players can know from what only the DM knows.
- Explain the design rationale for encounters, factions, and situations so the DM can adapt when players go off-script.
- Document what the DM may improvise within and what is constrained by canon.

---

## 3. Voice Model

**3.1 Functional clarity**
Every sentence answers a question the DM will actually have. "What does this NPC want?" "What will they not do under any circumstances?" "What happens if the players find the ledger before Kessara does?" If a sentence cannot be justified by a DM need, it should not be in DM-facing material. Background that does not change how the DM runs the encounter does not belong in operational notes; it belongs in a worldbuilding appendix.

**3.2 Behind-the-curtain directness**
State intentions, motivations, and consequences explicitly. Do not make the DM infer the villain's goal from an evocative description. Write "Marveth wants the Ledger because it proves the Duke's debts are forged. He will kill to prevent that proof from surfacing." State it. This is not a mystery to be revealed — it is a tool for the DM to run a coherent scene.

Label secrets explicitly. `[SECRET]` marks information the players cannot access. `[PUBLIC]` marks information characters in the world would commonly know. When information is conditionally available ("players who pass a DC 15 Insight check notice..."), note the condition and the reveal explicitly.

**3.3 Modularity**
DM-facing material should be skimmable during session prep — and during the session itself when a player does something unexpected. This means headers are navigation, not decoration. Key facts should not be buried in paragraph four of a character description. The most operationally critical information goes first.

If an NPC has a tripwire — a condition that changes their behavior dramatically — that tripwire should appear in the first section, bolded, not in a subordinate clause in the third paragraph.

**3.4 No dramatization**
Do not write DM notes in player-facing prose. "Kessara moves through the shadows like a ghost, her past written in the set of her jaw and the quiet fury behind her eyes..." is not DM-facing content. It is an atmospheric description that requires the DM to translate it into operational fact. Write: "Kessara: former spymaster, fired after the Ledger went missing, believes she was framed by Vael. She will use the party as tools and discard them the moment the Ledger is in her hands."

**3.5 Separation of knowledge states**
The DM always knows more than the players. DM-facing documents must be explicit about which facts are public (characters in-world know them), which are discoverable (players can learn them through action), and which are secret (only the DM knows, and the world will not reveal them unless specific conditions are met). Mixing knowledge states without labeling creates confusion and accidental spoilers.

---

## 4. Tone Profile

Professional collaborator. The writer is a colleague helping the DM run a better game. The tone is clear, knowledgeable, and direct — not performative, not atmospheric, not cautious. The DM does not need to be persuaded that the world is interesting; they are already invested. They need the information that lets them express that interest.

When design rationale is offered ("this encounter is designed to..."), state it plainly. The DM benefits from understanding the intent behind a design so they can preserve that intent when they adapt.

When something is left for the DM to decide, say so: "The nature of the Accord is deliberately ambiguous — DM should determine this based on the campaign's relationship with political intrigue." Do not leave things ambiguous without flagging the ambiguity; the DM needs to know what is fixed and what is flexible.

## 5. Sentence and Paragraph Structure

**Sentences:**
- Short declarative sentences preferred. "Kessara wants the Ledger. She will cooperate with the party until she has it."
- One fact per sentence for motivation, behavior, and constraints.
- Longer sentences acceptable for causal explanation: "The temple guards have been bribed by Vael, which is why they wave through anyone with a red token and challenge everyone without one."
- Active voice with explicit subjects: "Kessara will flee" not "it is expected that a retreat would be made."

**Paragraphs:**
- 3-5 sentences for contextual prose.
- Do not use long paragraphs for operational content. Break into headers and bullets.
- Each paragraph serves one operational function: motivation, history, mechanics, hooks, connections.

**Headers and subheaders:**
- Use liberally. Headers are how the DM navigates mid-session.
- Standard headers for NPC entries: Name / Role / Motivation / Limits / Secrets / Appearance / Tactics / Hooks
- Standard headers for faction entries: Overview / Goals / Methods / Leadership / Resources / Tensions / Player Hooks
- Standard headers for locations: Overview / Key Areas / NPCs Present / Encounters / Secrets / Hooks
- Bold key terms and tripwires that the DM must not miss.

**Lists:**
- Use bullets for: motivations, available resources, NPC connections, hooks, options.
- Use numbered lists for: sequences of events, conditional triggers ("if players do X, then Y, then Z").
- Use tables for: NPC comparison, faction relationship matrices, encounter options by level, stat blocks.

**Labels and callouts:**
- `[SECRET]` — information the players cannot access through normal means.
- `[PUBLIC]` — information any character in the world would know.
- `[IF DISCOVERED]` — information conditionally available to players, with discovery conditions stated.
- `[DM NOTE]` — design rationale or runtime guidance.
- `[CANON]` — information that is fixed and cannot be changed in adaptation.
- `[FLEXIBLE]` — information the DM may adapt without breaking the world.

## 6. Vocabulary Guidance

**Preferred:**
- Game-mechanical language used precisely: "advantage on Perception checks", "DC 14 Intimidation", "concentration spell", "short rest", "reaction"
- Motivation language: "wants", "fears", "will not", "will under condition X", "knows", "believes (incorrectly)"
- Limit language: "Kessara will not negotiate with Vael under any circumstances", "The Compact prevents open violence in the market district — faction agents operate through intermediaries"
- Conditional language: "if the players reveal X, then Y", "once the Ledger is recovered, the faction's behavior changes as follows"

**Avoid:**
- Atmospheric language that requires translation: "a woman haunted by her past" → "a woman who was wrongfully dismissed and intends to recover evidence of that wrong"
- Vague motivations: "interested in power" → "wants to control the grain trade in the eastern districts by acquiring the water rights held by the Merchants' Compact"
- Undefined limits: "won't do anything too drastic" → "will use blackmail and coercion; will not commit violence directly but will hire it"
- Passive attributions: "it is rumored that" — say who believes it and why, or use `[PUBLIC BELIEF]` as a label

## 7. Structural Preferences

**NPC entry structure:**
```
## [NPC Name]

**Role:** [What they are to the story / faction / location]
**Motivation:** [What they want, specifically]
**Limits:** [What they will not do. Non-negotiable constraints.]
**Fears:** [What threatens them, and how that changes their behavior]

[SECRET] [Any information the players cannot easily access]

**Appearance:** [How players experience them first. Physical and behavioral.]
**Tactics:** [How they behave in conflict, negotiation, or under pressure]

### Hooks
- [Specific way the NPC connects to the party or the larger story]
```

**Faction entry structure:**
```
## [Faction Name]

**Summary:** One sentence. What they are and what they do.

**Goals:** [Ordered by priority: immediate, medium-term, long-term]
**Methods:** [What they do to achieve goals. Specific, not general.]
**Resources:** [What they can bring to bear]
**Vulnerabilities:** [What exposes or weakens them]

[SECRET] [Hidden information — true leadership, actual agenda, etc.]

### Key Members
[Brief entry per major NPC with role, motivation, and faction relationship]

### Tensions
[Internal or external conflicts that create roleplay opportunity]

### Player Hooks
[Specific ways the party might get entangled with this faction]
```

**Encounter design entry structure:**
```
## [Encounter Name]

**Design intent:** [What this encounter is supposed to create / test / reveal]
**Context:** [When and where this encounter happens]

[DM NOTE] [Any flexibility guidance — what can be cut, moved, escalated]

### Setup
[What is in place before the players arrive]

### Triggers
[What starts the encounter, and optional alternate trigger conditions]

### Dynamics
[What the scene wants to do. What changes if players take specific actions.]

### Resolution Options
[How this can end. Multiple options preferred.]

### Consequences
[What follows from each resolution — affecting other encounters, NPCs, or the world state]
```

## 8. Anti-Patterns

**Writing DM notes in player-facing prose**
"Kessara, a mysterious and complex figure shrouded in ambiguity..." is not DM-facing content. The DM cannot run a character from an evocative description. They need the character's goal, their limit, their fear, and their tactics. State those explicitly. The atmospheric description belongs in the player-facing NPC impression text; the DM's version is the clinical file behind it.

**Hiding key operational facts in paragraph text**
If Kessara will betray the party the moment she gets the Ledger, that fact needs to be at the top of her entry, bolded or labeled, not buried in the second paragraph of her history. DMs running sessions under pressure scan for keywords. Information that requires careful reading to find will be missed.

**Omitting motivation and limits**
An NPC without explicit motivation is a puppet. An NPC without explicit limits is a deus ex machina. The motivation answers "what do they want and why?" The limits answer "what will they not do even to get what they want?" Without both, the DM cannot run the character consistently when players push in unexpected directions.

**Not labeling knowledge states**
Mixing player-accessible and DM-only information without labels forces the DM to parse which is which under session pressure. Every fact that differs between public and secret knowledge must be labeled. Do not assume the distinction is obvious from context.

**Encoding the adventure as a single path**
DM-facing design notes that present encounter resolution as a single linear path fail when players deviate — which they always will. Every encounter should document at least two resolution options and their downstream consequences. The design intent should be stated so the DM can preserve the intent even when the specific path is abandoned.

**Long background sections that don't change how the DM runs anything**
If the history of the faction does not change how the DM plays any NPC, runs any scene, or rules any decision, it does not belong in operational DM notes. It belongs in a worldbuilding appendix with a reference link. Operational notes should contain only what the DM needs to run the game.

## 9. Example Phrases

### Good

- "MOTIVATION: Kessara wants to recover the Ledger before Vael finds it. She will cooperate with the party up to the moment it is in hand — then she will attempt to flee."
- "[SECRET] The cult does not serve Thalax. Thalax has been dead for six years. The cult does not know this."
- "LIMITS: The Merchant's Compact will not commit open violence within the Freehold. All enforcement is through intermediaries, debt leverage, or legal obstruction. If pushed to direct confrontation, they will fold publicly and retaliate covertly."
- "DESIGN INTENT: This encounter is meant to force the party to choose between Kessara and the Compact. There is no correct answer. Both choices should have meaningful downstream costs."
- "[IF DISCOVERED: DC 14 Investigation or 1 hour search] The letter beneath the hearthstone is from Vael. It predates the Ledger's disappearance by three weeks."

### Bad

- "A mysterious and enigmatic figure who harbors many secrets, Kessara is a complex character with layers of personality that the DM can explore through roleplay."
- "The cult has an interesting history that the DM might want to develop. Their motives are deliberately left ambiguous to allow DM creativity."
- "Things could go in a lot of different directions here depending on what the players decide to do."
- "Vael is a dangerous opponent who should not be underestimated."
