# Style Pack: Lore — Player-Facing

**Domain:** Player-facing lore, world descriptions, item descriptions, quest text, faction descriptions, and setting introductions in D&D and worldbuilding contexts
**Version:** 1.0
**Last updated:** 2026-03-28
**Related guides:** `guides/style-packs/`, `guides/canon/`, `styles/lore-dm.md`

---

## 1. Domain Overview

Player-facing lore is the world as the players experience it. It appears in read-aloud text, item descriptions, tavern notices, books found in the world, faction pamphlets, location introductions, NPC first impressions, and any text meant to be received as if coming from inside the world rather than about it.

The defining constraint: the player has no prior knowledge. Every piece of player-facing text is building their mental model of the world from zero. The quality of that mental model — how vivid, specific, and inhabitable it feels — determines whether the world feels real or like a backdrop.

Player-facing lore does not explain. It evokes. It does not describe the world from outside; it speaks from inside it. The difference between museum placard prose and world prose is the difference between a world that exists and a world the player enters.

---

## 2. Reader and Purpose

**Reader:** A player at the table (or reading on a screen) who is encountering this part of the world for the first time. They have no knowledge of the lore beyond what they have already experienced in the game. They bring expectations from the fantasy genre, but those expectations should be confounded with specificity, not confirmed with generics.

**Purpose:** To establish atmosphere, create investment in the world, and give the player's imagination enough specific material to construct a real place, person, or thing. Not to front-load canon. Not to communicate mechanical information. Not to provide backstory the DM needs but the player does not.

The test: after reading this text, does the player feel somewhere specific, or do they feel somewhere generic?

---

## 3. Voice Model

**3.1 Evocative specificity**
Generic descriptions ("the ancient ruins") leave the reader with a generic image. Specific descriptions ("the towers had been cut at the base, not toppled — whoever did this was unhurried") place the reader inside a world with specific history. The specificity is not world-building data dump; it is the texture of a place that has been lived in or destroyed by something real.

Details should be chosen for implication, not completeness. The smell of a place tells more than its square footage. The single wrong thing in an otherwise normal scene creates more unease than a catalogue of horrors. Choose the detail that opens the door to the reader's imagination, then stop.

**3.2 Suggests more than it explains**
Good lore implies depth. The reader should feel that there is vastly more to know than what they have been told — and that this is enticing, not frustrating. Explain nothing that the world itself would not explain. A local would not say "the Ashmarked are an ancient order founded three hundred years ago by..." They would say "don't mark yourself unless you've settled your debts. The Ashmarked collect."

The technique: state a specific thing; omit the context that would explain it. The omission is what creates depth.

**3.3 Distinct world-voice**
The writing must feel like it comes from inside the world. This means no hedging language ("some might say", "legend has it" — unless mystery is the intended effect), no anachronisms unless the world's tone specifically permits them, and no genre vocabulary that punctures the world-frame ("this dungeon", "your party", "as adventurers").

World-voice varies by project: a dark gothic world has a different voice from a folkloric fairy-tale world. Establish the project's tonal register in the project style notes and maintain it. But within any register, the voice must feel indigenous to the world — not imposed from outside.

**3.4 Present-tense immersion or past-tense gravitas**
Choose tense for effect, not by default. Present tense places the reader in the moment: "The fire is still going. Someone left in a hurry." Past tense creates the weight of history: "The garrison held for three days. Then it didn't." Never the neutral present tense of a reference document: "The city is a major trade hub located in the northern province."

**3.5 Leave room for the reader's imagination**
Do not complete the picture. Sketch the edge of it. The best world prose gives the reader five specific details and lets them build the rest. Over-described worlds feel smaller because the reader has nothing to add. Under-described worlds feel thin. The calibration is: be specific enough that the reader has something to work with; stop before you've decided everything for them.

## 4. Tone Profile

Tone is project-specific. Set the tonal register in project style notes. Common registers:

- **Dark/gothic:** Dry, stark, understated. Horror through implication rather than description. What is absent is more disturbing than what is present.
- **Epic/mythic:** Weighty, sparse. Short sentences carry enormous events. The prose should feel like translated verse.
- **Folkloric:** Conversational but strange. The normal and the uncanny described with equal calm.
- **Whimsical:** Light, precise, gently wrong. The humor is in the specificity of the absurd.

Regardless of register: the text must be immersive and specific. An epistolary text found in the world has the voice of the character who wrote it. A DM's read-aloud text has the voice of the world observing itself. A faction pamphlet has the voice of that faction's rhetoric. Every text has a source inside the world; write from that source.

## 5. Sentence and Paragraph Structure

**Sentences:**
- Fragments are acceptable and often preferred. "The smell of something burned. And beneath it, something older." The fragment creates a different rhythm than the complete sentence and can land harder.
- Short punchy sentences for impact moments. The shorter the sentence, the more weight it carries — use this deliberately.
- Longer atmospheric sentences for world-setting. Build the place with a longer sentence, then land the detail in a short one.
- Varied rhythm is essential. Monotone sentence length — all short or all long — removes the pacing that makes prose feel like a world rather than a list.

**Paragraphs:**
- Paced, not encyclopedic. A paragraph of pure information delivery is reference material, not lore.
- Location descriptions: 2-4 sentences for short (room, item), 4-8 for extended (district, landmark).
- Read-aloud text: no longer than a DM can deliver before the players need to respond. 60-100 words is a practical maximum for read-aloud.
- Do not cram multiple distinct sensory registers into one sentence. One thing at a time, let it land, then the next.

**Formatting:**
- Italics for texts found in the world (books, notices, letters).
- Plain text for DM read-aloud.
- No headers within flavor text — headers are DM-facing structure.
- No bullet points within flavor text. Lists break the immersion register.

---

## 6. Vocabulary Guidance

**Preferred:**
- World-specific vocabulary over generic fantasy tropes. If the world has a name for its wandering spirit-monks, use it; don't call them "monks."
- Sensory specificity: smell, texture, sound, temperature — not just visual.
- Strong verbs: "the floor had given way", "the fire had jumped to the rafters", "the water ran black"
- Concrete nouns with weight: "the debt", "the third bell", "a coin of the old mint"
- Dialogue or voice that sounds native to its character

**Avoid:**
- `ancient`, `mystical`, `powerful`, `legendary`, `magical` — these are placeholders for specifics. Replace every one with what is actually old, strange, strong, notable, or enchanted, and how.
- `adventurer`, `dungeon`, `quest`, `loot` — these are meta-game terms that break world-voice. Use world-specific equivalents.
- `some say`, `legend has it`, `it is said` — unless mystery is the intended effect. Used reflexively, these phrases drain authority from the writing.
- Anachronisms in non-modern settings: "budget", "logistics", "infrastructure", "protocol" — unless the world's register specifically permits contemporary language.
- Hedging language: "a somewhat foreboding atmosphere", "rather imposing", "quite old" — commit to the description.
- Passive constructions that create distance: "the area was said to be..." — say it directly or attribute it specifically.

## 7. Structural Preferences

**Location descriptions — short (room, clearing, vehicle):**
1. Immediate sensory impression (what hits first)
2. Key visual details (2-3 specific things, not a catalogue)
3. The detail that changes the meaning of the space

**Location descriptions — extended (district, landmark, wilderness area):**
1. Atmosphere and approach (what travelers experience arriving)
2. The dominant feature
3. Specific telling detail that reveals history or character
4. What is absent or wrong

**Item descriptions:**
1. What it is, briefly (the category)
2. What it looks like — one specific, non-generic detail
3. What it means or implies — the thing the art cannot show

**NPC descriptions (read-aloud first impressions):**
1. First impression — what the players notice immediately
2. One distinguishing physical or behavioral detail
3. The thing that creates a question ("why is she watching the door?")
Do not explain the NPC's history or motivation in read-aloud text. That is DM-facing information.

**Faction descriptions (player-facing):**
1. What they are known for (from the outside)
2. Their stance toward the world (what they want, what they do)
3. What encountering them feels like
Not their internal politics, their founding history, or their secret goals — those are DM-facing.

## 8. Anti-Patterns

**Opening with history before mood**
History without atmosphere is database entry, not lore. Never begin player-facing text with "Founded in the year 412 by..." or "The kingdom was once..." Begin with what the place feels like to be in. History can emerge from specific details.

**Museum placard prose**
"The Ash Temple is a notable example of pre-Sundering architecture, characterized by its distinctive use of volcanic stone..." This voice exists outside the world looking in. It belongs in a DM reference document, not in a player's experience. Write from inside.

**"Adventurers know that..."**
The player-facing text is the first encounter. The player does not know what adventurers know. Do not address the reader as a genre participant ("as any seasoned traveler can tell you..."). The reader is arriving at this world for the first time, in this moment.

**Generic fantasy descriptors without specifics**
"An ancient and powerful artifact of great mystical significance" means nothing. Replace every generic with the specific thing that makes it ancient, powerful, and significant. What does it weigh? What does it smell like? What does it do to the air around it? What did it cost the last person who used it?

**Info-dumping canon the player doesn't need yet**
Player-facing lore delivers what the player needs for this moment. The three-hundred-year history of the Ember Throne is not needed the moment the player walks into the throne room. The weight of the silence and the single burned banner are. Trust the reader to want more; do not preemptively satisfy the want.

**Completing the picture**
A world described in total leaves nowhere for the imagination to live. The gaps are not absence — they are invitation. The best lore writing is half-finished on purpose.

## 9. Example Phrases

### Good

- "The Ashmarked don't pray. They bargain, and the things they bargain with do not speak in words."
- "Three years after the Sundering, Velrath is still burning. The smoke is what travelers smell first, a day's ride before they see the walls."
- "The door is unlocked. The lock is broken from the inside."
- "She doesn't look at you when she speaks. She looks at the place where the fire was."
- "The market sells everything. Everything here has been sold before."
- "A coin from the old mint: still spends. The face on it has been dead for sixty years."

### Bad

- "The ancient and mystical order of powerful warriors known as the Ashmarked is a group that has existed for centuries and is known throughout the land for their formidable abilities and enigmatic practices."
- "Players who visit this location will find a dungeon filled with interesting creatures and valuable loot."
- "As adventurers know, the Sundering was a cataclysmic event that fundamentally changed the world as they know it."
- "This powerful magical item is extremely rare and highly sought after by many collectors and magic users."
- "Legend has it that the city was once a great center of civilization, though some say the legends are exaggerated."
