# Style Pack: Card Flavor Text

**Domain:** Trading card game and collectible card game flavor text — brief narrative or atmospheric text appearing on game cards
**Version:** 1.0
**Last updated:** 2026-03-28
**Related guides:** `guides/style-packs/`, `styles/lore-player-facing.md`, `doctrine/compression.md`

---

## 1. Domain Overview

Flavor text is the smallest unit of world-building in print. It appears beneath the rules text on a game card — typically 100-200 characters, sometimes as few as 40. It does no mechanical work. It exists entirely to make the card feel like part of a world that existed before the game did and will exist after the game ends.

The medium is extreme. A novelist uses 80,000 words to establish a world. Flavor text uses fifteen. The compression required is not a constraint to work around — it is the form itself. Every technique of the form exists in service of maximum impact per character.

Flavor text fails when it could belong to any card, any world, any moment. It succeeds when it belongs to exactly this card, in exactly this world, at exactly this instant.

---

## 2. Reader and Purpose

**Reader:** A player holding the card during a game. They have two to three seconds. They are not studying the text — they are registering it. Flavor text that requires a second reading to understand has failed. Flavor text that lands on the first pass and rewards a second is the gold standard.

**Secondary purpose:** Flavor text accumulates. A player who sees fifty cards from one set has read fifty fragments of a world. The aggregate of those fragments should cohere into a world-state: a history, a crisis, a set of stakes, a cast of voices. No single piece of flavor text carries the weight of world-building, but no single piece of flavor text is exempt from it.

---

## 3. Voice Model

**3.1 Maximum compression**
Every word must justify its position. "The fire remembers what it burns" is seven words. Each word is necessary; none could be removed without destroying the line. Test every word: if the line still works without it, cut it. The length limit is not the enemy — slack within the limit is.

**3.2 One clear image or implication**
Flavor text that tries to do two things does neither well. One image: the door, the debt, the moment before. One implication: what was lost, what was done, what the world now knows. Identify the one thing this flavor text adds to the card's identity and build the entire line around that one thing.

**3.3 World-weight**
Fifteen words should feel like a fragment of something enormous. This is achieved not by claiming enormity ("the greatest battle ever fought") but by specificity that implies context. "She didn't betray the king. She just stopped pretending to serve him." This line implies a relationship, a decision, a history, a world — without naming any of it. The implication is the weight.

**3.4 The frame is the frame**
The art tells one story. The flavor text does not re-describe the art. It adds one thing the art cannot: a voice, a consequence, a contradiction, a prior moment, a future implication. A card showing a fire does not need "the flames rose high" in the flavor text — the art already shows that. The flavor text adds what the art withholds.

**3.5 Memorable after one reading**
The test: can the player quote it, approximately, an hour later? If not, it was not distinct enough. The memorable line achieves distinctiveness through an unexpected word, an unusual structure, or an implication that the reader has to briefly hold — long enough to hold it, short enough that it resolves cleanly.

---

## 4. Tone Profile

Tone is card-specific. The flavor text should match the mechanical and thematic register of the card:

- **Removal spells:** often dry, surgical, final. "The problem was solved. The solution was worse."
- **Combat spells:** kinetic, terse, often from the perspective of the defeated. "She had time to be surprised."
- **Dark or cursed artifacts:** clinical precision that implies wrongness. "It works exactly as intended."
- **Heroic cards:** wry rather than triumphant. Triumph is what happens; the flavor captures the cost or the irony.
- **Enchantments and spells:** mythic, slightly alien. The grammar can be strange if it is consistently strange.
- **Creature cards:** often the creature's voice, or a witness's voice. The creature's perspective is more interesting than an external description.

The tonal register is project-specific and should be established in project style notes. Within a project, maintain tonal consistency: flavor text in a grim world should not occasionally veer into cheerfulness.

## 5. Sentence and Paragraph Structure

**Sentence count:**
- One sentence, strongly preferred.
- Two sentences permitted only if they create a before/after structure, a call-and-response, or a tension and its resolution.
- Three sentences is almost always wrong. At three sentences, you are writing an epigraph, not flavor text.

**Sentence structure:**
- Fragments acceptable and often preferred: "The door had three locks. The last one was a question." The second "sentence" is not grammatically complete. It does not need to be.
- No explanatory clauses. Flavor text has no budget for "which means that", "in other words", or "as a result."
- Inverted structures create rhythm: "Dead she may be, but she votes." Normal word order is default; inversion is a tool for emphasis, used once per card, at most.

**Quotations:**
- Attribution (speaker's name, title, or context) follows the text, set off typographically.
- The speaker should be relevant to the card's mechanic or story moment — not a random wise-sounding sage.
- The quote should feel like something the speaker would actually say, in that speaker's register.
- Quotations and narrative flavor are different formats. Do not mix them in a single card.

**Narrative (non-attributed) flavor:**
- Present tense preferred: "the door opens once." Immediacy.
- Past tense for historical weight: "the last of the Accord died here." Gravity.
- Never future tense as a statement of fact: "the world will remember" — do not make predictions from inside the flavor text.

---

## 6. Vocabulary Guidance

**Preferred:**
- Unusual specific nouns that carry world-weight: "the debt", "the third bell", "the surveyor's mark", "the left eye of the Compact"
- Active verbs that do work: "holds", "forgets", "costs", "waits", "breaks"
- Strong monosyllabic words: "debt", "scar", "dusk", "spent", "lock"
- Invented world-terms used without definition — if a word is evocative and the reader does not need a definition to get the feeling, use it

**Avoid:**
- Empty modifier adjectives: `ancient`, `mystical`, `powerful`, `legendary`, `sacred` — these words are promises the surrounding line must keep. If the line cannot keep the promise, remove the adjective. If it can, replace the adjective with the specific thing that makes it ancient, powerful, or sacred.
- Simile constructions: "like a river that...", "as cold as..." — simile requires real estate the format does not have, and almost always lands weaker than a direct statement
- Overlong conjunctions and subordination: "although it had been said that..." — cut to the claim
- Generic epic sentiment: "For glory! For the realm!" — this is the sound of a card with nothing to say
- Dialogue tags on attributed quotes: *"The world has room for one more mistake," said the Archivist.* — the attribution format handles speaker identification; do not double up

**Character count discipline:**
Most games impose hard limits of 140-200 characters including spaces and attribution. Write to fit. Count characters before finalizing. If a line requires 220 characters and every word is necessary, revise the structure — find different words that achieve the same effect in fewer characters.

## 7. Structural Preferences

**Attributed quote format:**
```
"[Flavor text in quotation marks.]"
—[Speaker name, title or descriptor]
```
The attribution is typeset separately in game. Write it as a distinct line. The speaker's name should be specific: "—Marveth, Third Warden of Golm" not "—a wise man."

**Narrative flavor format:**
```
[Flavor text. No quotation marks. One or two sentences.]
```
Third person or second person. First person without attribution is disorienting.

**Conditional format (before/after):**
Two-sentence structure where the second sentence recontextualizes the first.
```
The vault had not been opened in forty years.
It had not been closed, either.
```

**Voice format (creature speaking):**
```
"[First-person voice of the creature or entity.]"
```
No attribution needed if the card is the speaker. Use sparingly — not every creature needs a voice.

## 8. Anti-Patterns

**Explaining what the card does mechanically**
"When creatures attack, they feel the pull of ancient magic that..." The mechanics are on the card. Flavor text is not a tooltip. It does not explain or reinforce the rules text. It belongs to a different register entirely.

**Generic epic sentiment**
"For the glory of the kingdom!" "We will never yield!" "Stand firm, brave warriors!" These lines have no world-weight because they could appear on any card in any game. They are the sound of nothing being said with enthusiasm.

**Too many syllables destroying the rhythm**
Flavor text is read aloud in the reader's head. Count the syllables. A line of eight words that is ten syllables reads faster and lands harder than a line of eight words that is sixteen syllables. Prefer short syllable counts in strong-position words (the end of the line, the subject noun, the key verb).

**Flavor that could belong to any card**
If you could swap the flavor text between two different cards and neither card would suffer, the flavor text has failed. Flavor text earns its place by belonging to this card: this mechanic, this creature, this moment in this world's history.

**The obvious reading**
If the art shows fire and the flavor text says "fire destroys," you have not added anything. The flavor text should add information the art withholds — a voice, a context, an implication, a prior event, a future consequence. If you can get the same information from looking at the art, the flavor text is not doing its job.

**Hedging and qualification**
"It was said that perhaps the door had once been...": this is a line that is afraid of itself. Flavor text must commit. If the door was a question, say "the door was a question." If that seems too strong, find a different image that you are willing to commit to.

## 9. Example Phrases

### Good

- "She didn't betray the king. She just stopped pretending to serve him."
- "The door had three locks. The last one was a question."
- *"Fire remembers what it burns."* — Pyromancer's saying
- "The debt was forgiven. The forgiveness was not."
- "It works exactly as intended."
- *"I have made worse deals."* —Kessara, former Third Warden
- "The tower fell on a Tuesday. The city noticed the following week."
- "Free. That was the word they used. No one defined it."

### Bad

- "This powerful ancient spell is known to legendary sorcerers who have mastered the mystical arts of elemental fire magic."
- "For the glory of the kingdom, we shall never yield!" —Generic Hero
- "When creatures attack, they are empowered by the ancient magic of the realm, gaining strength from the battlefield."
- "Like a river that finds its way through every obstacle, the magic always returns to its source."
- "It is said that perhaps long ago, in the time before memory, the world was different."
