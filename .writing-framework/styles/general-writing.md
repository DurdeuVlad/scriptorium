# Style Pack: General Writing

**Domain:** General non-fiction writing for intelligent adult readers
**Version:** 1.0
**Last updated:** 2026-03-28
**Related guides:** `guides/style-packs/`, `doctrine/compression.md`, `doctrine/voice.md`

---

## 1. Domain Overview

This style pack governs non-fiction writing that does not belong to a specialized domain — essays, commentary, long-form articles, opinion pieces, explainers, and narrative non-fiction. The work appears in publications, on websites, and in documents where clarity and credibility are the primary assets.

General writing is not a weaker version of specialized writing. It is harder. Without domain conventions to lean on, every sentence earns its place through quality alone.

---

## 2. Reader and Purpose

**Reader:** An intelligent adult with limited time and no patience for condescension. They can follow complex ideas. They will not struggle through bad prose to find a point that could have been stated plainly.

**Purpose:** To inform, argue, or illuminate — and to leave the reader with a clear understanding of what you claimed and why it holds.

The reader is the constraint. Write for the reader you want, not the reader you fear.

---

## 3. Voice Model

**3.1 Direct and confident**
State the claim. If you are uncertain, say so precisely — "the evidence is thin" is more honest and more useful than "it might be possible that perhaps." Hedging without reason is noise. Reserve qualifications for genuine uncertainty, and make them specific.

**3.2 Specific over abstract**
Abstract claims float. Specific claims land. Do not write "many organizations struggle with this problem." Write "the company missed three consecutive deadlines because no one owned the decision." Specificity is not decoration — it is the argument itself.

**3.3 Compression as discipline**
Every sentence must do work. If a sentence repeats what the prior sentence said, cut it. If a paragraph circles back to its own beginning, cut the beginning. Tight prose is not cold prose — it is prose that trusts the reader to carry what they have already received.

**3.4 Varied rhythm**
Long analytical sentences build a proposition. Short sentences detonate it. Vary the length deliberately. Three long sentences in a row will numb the reader. Three short sentences in a row will sound like a list. Alternate. Use short sentences for emphasis, not as a stylistic tic.

**3.5 Trust the reader**
Do not explain what readers can infer. Do not define words the reader knows. Do not summarize what you just said. If the writing is doing its job, the reader has understood; repeating it signals that you don't believe they did.

---

## 4. Tone Profile

Clear, engaged, occasionally wry. Not academic — no unnecessary subordination or passive constructions that exist to sound formal. Not casual — no slang, no hedging behind informality, no false intimacy.

The model is a smart person talking to another smart person who has not yet thought about this problem. The writer knows more on this specific topic. The reader is not impressed by the writer's credentials — only by the argument.

Wry when it fits. A dry observation or a light contradiction lands better than labored wit. Humor is punctuation, not content.

---

## 5. Sentence and Paragraph Structure

**Sentences:**
- Mix complex and simple constructions. Complex sentences build; simple sentences resolve.
- Avoid three long sentences in a row. After two complex sentences, land a short one.
- Active voice is default. Passive voice is permitted when the receiver of the action is more important than the actor, or when the actor is genuinely unknown.
- Front-load the subject and verb. Don't subordinate the main clause.

**Paragraphs:**
- 1 sentence minimum; 5 sentences typical maximum.
- Each paragraph advances the argument by one step. If two consecutive paragraphs say the same thing at different lengths, cut one.
- The first sentence of a paragraph is load-bearing. If the reader only reads the first sentence of each paragraph, they should understand the shape of the argument.
- Do not end paragraphs with throat-clearing transitions. The next paragraph begins a new point; it does not need introduction.

**Headers:**
- Use for long pieces (over 600 words) when sections are genuinely distinct.
- Do not use headers for short pieces. Headers in a 400-word essay are scaffolding left behind by a builder who forgot to remove it.
- Headers describe the section's content, not its function ("The cost of delay" not "Discussion").

**Lists:**
- Use only when order matters (sequence) or parallelism matters (comparison, enumeration).
- Do not convert continuous reasoning into bullets. Reasoning requires connective tissue that bullets remove.
- A list of two items is not a list. Two items are a sentence.

## 6. Vocabulary Guidance

**Preferred:**
- Concrete nouns over abstract ones ("the meeting" over "the engagement")
- Active verbs that carry meaning ("collapsed", "refused", "broke")
- Direct phrasing ("because" over "due to the fact that", "if" over "in the event that")
- Plain Anglo-Saxon where available ("use" over "utilize", "help" over "facilitate")

**Avoid:**
- `utilize` — use "use" unless you mean putting something to a purpose it was not designed for
- `leverage` — unless referring to mechanical or financial leverage; otherwise use "use", "exploit", or "apply"
- `robust` — overused, usually empty; say what makes the thing strong
- `holistic` — almost always meaningless; say what you mean
- `comprehensive` — say what it covers instead
- `cutting-edge` — marketing language; describe what is actually new or advanced
- `paradigm` — almost never needed; use "model", "framework", "approach"
- `synergy` — meaningless in non-technical context; describe the actual effect
- `ecosystem` — acceptable in specific technical/biological contexts; banned as a vague organizational metaphor
- `space` — banned as a synonym for "market" or "field" ("we operate in the fintech space")
- `journey` — banned outside literal travel
- `impactful` — not a word; use "significant", "effective", or name the impact

**Never:**
- `delve into` — in any context
- `it's worth noting` — if it's worth noting, note it; this phrase adds no information
- `in today's world` / `in today's rapidly changing landscape` — banned entirely
- `in conclusion` — if you need to signal the end, you haven't written an ending
- `needless to say` — then don't say it
- `at the end of the day` — cliché with no content
- `going forward` — use "from now on" or name the timeline

## 7. Structural Preferences

**Lead with the point, not the setup.**
The first paragraph establishes what the piece is about and why the reader should continue. It does not warm up. It does not provide background. It does not define terms. The argument begins in sentence one.

**Background when necessary, not by convention.**
Context is not automatically required. Provide it only when the reader cannot follow the argument without it. Place it as close as possible to where it becomes relevant — not in the introduction.

**No summary at section ends.**
If the section is well-written, summarizing it is redundant. If it is not well-written, summarizing it does not fix the problem.

**The ending makes a claim, not a gesture.**
Do not end with "time will tell" or "there are no easy answers." The ending earns its place by saying something — a final implication, an unresolved tension made explicit, a provocation. Endings that trail off are unfinished pieces.

## 8. Anti-Patterns

**Over-explaining transitions**
"Now that we've established X, let's look at Y" is not a transition — it's a signpost that the writer is unsure the reader is following. Good structure makes transitions implicit. If you need to recap what you just said before moving on, tighten the structure.

**Opening with a definition**
"Trust, as Merriam-Webster defines it, is..." signals that the writer had nothing to say in the first paragraph. Definitions are reference material, not arguments. Start with the claim or the case.

**Three examples when one strong one suffices**
One concrete, specific example proves a point. Three examples suggest the writer is not confident in any of them. When in doubt, develop one example fully rather than mentioning three in passing.

**Throat-clearing first paragraphs**
A paragraph that explains what the piece will cover without actually beginning the argument is a placeholder. "This essay will examine..." "In this article, I will explore..." Cut the first paragraph and see whether the second is where the actual piece starts. Often it is.

**Escalating vagueness at the end**
Pieces that begin specifically and grow vaguer as they proceed have been abandoned rather than concluded. The final paragraphs require the same discipline as the first.

**Padding through synonyms**
"The result was swift, quick, and immediate." Compression means choosing one word and trusting it.

## 9. Example Phrases

### Good

- "The problem isn't complexity — it's that nobody defined the boundary."
- "Cut the first paragraph. The second is where the actual point starts."
- "Three departments reviewed the proposal. None of them owned it."
- "The approach worked. It also cost them every engineer who had built it."
- "This is not a new problem. It is a problem that has been renamed."

### Bad

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape, it's more important than ever to leverage cutting-edge solutions..."
- "This section will explore the various ways in which the concept of trust manifests across different organizational contexts."
- "At the end of the day, there are no easy answers, and time will tell how these challenges unfold going forward."
- "It's worth noting that the data, while not comprehensive, does provide a holistic view of the paradigm shift currently underway."
- "Needless to say, the results speak for themselves."
